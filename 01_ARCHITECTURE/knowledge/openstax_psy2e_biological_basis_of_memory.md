## 2. Concepte Extrase și Dovezi Textuale Verificate

### 1. Engram and Lashley's Equipotentiality Hypothesis
Karl Lashley searched for the physical representation of memory, called the engram, by lesioning rat brains; failing to find a single location, he proposed that other parts of a brain area can compensate for damage to that area.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "Based on his creation of lesions and the animals’ reaction, he formulated the equipotentiality hypothesis : if part of one area of the brain involved in memory is damaged, another part of the same area can take over that memory function (Lashley, 1950)."

- **Termeni cheie**: engram, equipotentiality hypothesis, lesions, Karl Lashley

### 2. Role of the Amygdala in Emotional Memory and Consolidation
The amygdala regulates emotions such as fear and plays a key role in memory consolidation, facilitating deeper encoding when experiences are emotionally arousing.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "Because of its role in processing emotional information, the amygdala is also involved in memory consolidation: the process of transferring new learning into long-term memory. The amygdala seems to facilitate encoding memories at a deeper level when the event is emotionally arousing."

- **Termeni cheie**: amygdala, memory consolidation, fear, encoding

### 3. Role of the Hippocampus and Declarative Memory in Patient H.M.
The hippocampus is vital for normal recognition and spatial memory as well as consolidating declarative memories; damage or bilateral removal, as in patient H.M., leaves an individual unable to form new semantic and explicit memories while past memories remain intact.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "One famous patient, known for years only as H. M., had both his left and right temporal lobes (hippocampi) removed in an attempt to help control the seizures he had been suffering from for years (Corkin, Amaral, González, Johnson, & Hyman, 1997). As a result, his declarative memory was significantly affected, and he could not form new semantic knowledge."

- **Termeni cheie**: hippocampus, declarative memory, semantic knowledge, H. M.

### 4. Role of the Cerebellum in Procedural Memory
While explicit memory processing depends heavily on the hippocampus, implicit memories such as procedural memory and classically conditioned responses are processed by the cerebellum.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "Although the hippocampus seems to be more of a processing area for explicit memories, you could still lose it and be able to create implicit memories (procedural memory, motor learning, and classical conditioning), thanks to your cerebellum ( Figure 8.8 )."

- **Termeni cheie**: cerebellum, implicit memories, procedural memory, classical conditioning

### 5. Prefrontal Cortex Activation in Encoding and Retrieval
Brain imaging reveals that different regions of the prefrontal cortex manage different memory processes, with semantic processing and encoding linked to the left frontal region and retrieval linked to the right frontal region.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "In another study, encoding was associated with left frontal activity, while retrieval of information was associated with the right frontal region (Craik et al., 1999)."

- **Termeni cheie**: prefrontal cortex, encoding, retrieval, PET scans

### 6. Synaptic Plasticity and Neurotransmitters in Memory Consolidation
Communication among neurons via neurotransmitters is essential for memory formation, where repeated neuronal activity leads to increased neurotransmitters and more efficient synaptic connections.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "Repeated activity by neurons leads to increased neurotransmitters in the synapses and more efficient and more synaptic connections. This is how memory consolidation occurs."

- **Termeni cheie**: neurotransmitters, synapses, synaptic connections, memory consolidation

### 7. Arousal Theory and Flashbulb Memories
Arousal theory posits that strong emotional experiences elicit the release of neurotransmitters and hormones that strengthen memory traces, generating exceptionally vivid recollections known as flashbulb memories.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "It is also believed that strong emotions trigger the formation of strong memories, and weaker emotional experiences form weaker memories; this is called arousal theory (Christianson, 1992). For example, strong emotional experiences can trigger the release of neurotransmitters, as well as hormones, which strengthen memory; therefore, our memory for an emotional event is usually better than our memory for a non-emotional event."

- **Termeni cheie**: arousal theory, neurotransmitters, hormones, flashbulb memory

### 8. Frailties of Flashbulb Memories and Memory Reconstruction
Despite high subjective clarity, flashbulb memories are subject to memory decay and distortion over time because memories are reconstructed from disparate brain locations rather than replayed like a recording.

> **Citat Verificat Sursă**:
> "Even flashbulb memories for important events can have decreased accuracy with the passage of time."

- **Termeni cheie**: flashbulb memories, accuracy, memory reconstruction
